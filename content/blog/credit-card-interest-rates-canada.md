## The Short Answer

If you pay your full statement balance every month, a credit card's interest rate costs you nothing. If you don't, it is usually the most expensive thing about the card — more than the annual fee, and often more than the card earns back in rewards.

Of the 120 cards in our catalogue, **114 publish a purchase interest rate**. Of those, **87 charge essentially the same high rate** — the standard rate on most Canadian cards. Only **9 are genuinely low-interest cards**.

Interest rates change, so always confirm the current rate on the issuer's page before you apply.

## How Credit Card Interest Works in Canada

The interest rate on a card is an annual rate, but it is charged daily. On a standard high-interest card, a balance costs you roughly **60 cents a day for every $1,000** you carry.

Two rules decide whether you pay any of it:

- **Pay the full statement balance by the due date and you pay no interest on purchases.** Canadian cards give you a grace period of at least 21 days on new purchases, as long as the full balance is paid.
- **Carry any balance past the due date and the grace period goes.** On most cards, interest is then charged on your purchases from the day you made them, not from the due date.

The rate we compare is the **purchase rate**. Cash advances and balance transfers usually carry their own, higher rates, and cash advances generally start charging interest the same day, with no grace period at all.

## What a Standard Rate Costs in Dollars

Interest is easier to judge in dollars than in percentages. Here is roughly what an average balance costs over a full year on a standard card compared with the low-interest cards further down:

| Average balance carried | Standard high-interest card | Typical $29 low-rate card | Lowest-rate no-fee cards |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $500 | about $110 | about $70 | about $55 |
| $1,000 | about $220 | about $140 | about $110 |
| $2,500 | about $550 | about $350 | about $275 |
| $5,000 | about $1,100 | about $700 | about $550 |

These are rounded, simple-interest estimates, and your own rate may differ. Interest added to an unpaid balance starts earning interest itself, so the real cost of a balance you never pay down is slightly higher.

## Interest Cancels Rewards Fast

This is the part most people miss. Rewards are earned on spending; interest is charged on the balance. When you carry a balance, the second number grows much faster than the first.

Say you spend **$1,500 a month** on a card that pays **2% cash back**. That could earn you about **$360 a year**.

On a standard high-interest card, carrying an average balance of about **$1,640** costs roughly the same $360 in interest. Carry more than that and the card costs you money, however good its rewards are. Carry $5,000 and you pay around $1,100 in interest to earn $360 back.

So the order of priority is simple. If you carry a balance, the interest rate matters more than the earn rate. If you pay in full, the earn rate matters and the interest rate does not.

## When a Low-Rate Card Pays for Its Fee

A low-rate card with a fee is worth it when the interest it saves is bigger than the fee.

The $29 cards charge roughly a third less interest than a standard card. Carrying an average balance of about **$363** over the year saves enough interest to cover the $29.

The same maths applies to the premium cards, at a much bigger number. The Scotiabank Platinum Amex charges less than half the standard rate, but on interest savings alone it only covers its $399 fee once you carry an average of about **$3,325**. Below that, you would need its rewards and benefits to make up the difference.

## The Highest Rates

At the other end, three Capital One cards charge the highest interest in our catalogue, well above the standard rate: the [Smart Rewards Mastercard](/credit-cards/capital-one-smart-rewards), the [Guaranteed Mastercard](/credit-cards/capital-one-guaranteed) and the [Guaranteed Secured Mastercard](/credit-cards/capital-one-secured). These are cards for building or rebuilding credit, and they are designed for people who may find it harder to get approved elsewhere.

That is a fair trade if you use them as intended: small purchases, paid off in full every month, to build a payment history. Carrying a balance on one of them is the costliest way to borrow on this list — $1,000 carried for a year costs about **$300**.

## If You Are Carrying a Balance Now

A few practical steps, in order of impact:

1. **Stop adding to it.** New purchases on a card with a balance usually start charging interest straight away. Use a different card or debit for day-to-day spending until the balance is gone.
2. **Pay more than the minimum.** Minimum payments are designed to keep you paying for a long time. Your statement shows an estimate of how long the balance would take to clear on minimum payments alone — it is worth reading once.
3. **Consider a lower rate.** Moving to one of the cards above, or using a balance transfer offer, can cut the cost while you pay it down. Balance transfers usually come with a fee and a promotional rate that ends, so check both before you move.

## How to Use This

Start with one question: do you pay your card off in full every month?

If yes, ignore the interest rate and choose on rewards. The [rewards calculator](/credit-card-calculator-canada) estimates what cards could earn from your actual spending.

If no, the interest rate is the number that matters most. A lower-rate card can save you more than most rewards cards earn, and the list above is the place to start. Confirm the current rate with the issuer before you apply.
